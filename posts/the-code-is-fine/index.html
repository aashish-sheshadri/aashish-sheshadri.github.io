The Code Is Fine. That Is the Problem.

Generated code is correct at the level anyone reviews it. The failures that matter live one level up.
August 14, 2026 · 14 min · Aashish Sheshadri

“I asked Claude and this is what it told me.”

I have heard some version of that sentence a lot this year, and my reaction to it has changed. The first few times, I heard it as a confession — a slightly sheepish admission that the author had not worked something out alone. Now I hear it the way I would hear someone say they looked something up.

What bothers me is that I usually cannot argue with the code.

The Code Is Fine#

Actually fine. Idiomatic, defensive in the right places, better commented than what I was writing at the same career stage. If the question is whether this diff should merge, the answer is yes.

There is a version of this essay where a senior engineer complains that the youth have it easy. I am not writing that one, and not out of politeness — I use these tools constantly, and when I am three layers into a library I have never read, asking is faster than grepping and the answer is usually right.

So the problem sits somewhere less convenient than the tool or the person. We have industrialized the production of correct artifacts. Correctness at the level of the artifact was never what made software safe to ship.

The Question You Did Not Ask#

Ask a model for a retry policy. You will get exponential backoff with jitter, which is correct, and a short explanation of why, which is also correct. Nothing about that answer is wrong.

Now put it in every client of a service that is having a bad afternoon. Each client is politely backing off. Each is also issuing two or three times the requests it would have, and if three layers of the stack independently decided to be polite in the same way, the multiplication compounds. The service does not recover when the triggering problem clears, because the retries have become the problem. Bronson and colleagues gave this a name in a 2021 HotOS paper — metastable failure — drawn, in their words, from a decade of operating hyperscale distributed systems.

The same shape shows up wherever many correct things interact. A cache is a cache until a million keys expire in the same second because they were all populated by the same overnight job. A health check is a health check until it verifies its dependencies, at which point one sick service pulls down every fleet that checks it.

A good model will often volunteer some of this — retry budgets, circuit breakers, staggered TTLs — because those caveats are in the literature it learned from. What it cannot tell you is that two other layers of your stack already retry, that your cache warms in a single batch job at 4am, or which of your services share a dependency and will therefore fail their health checks together.

That is the part I do not think better models close. You could put the system in the prompt, if you knew which part of it mattered. Nobody I know has that written down for their own stack, myself included.

The Model Was Always Distributed#

The tempting conclusion is that engineers used to carry this system model and are losing it, and that generated code is what took it away. I have caught myself making that argument. I now think it puts the model in the wrong place.

No individual held the composed picture of a few thousand interacting components. Not the principal engineers, whose job description came closest to it, and not the people who had been on every incident call. There were overlapping partial models, each held by someone who had been burned by a different subset, and a process slow enough that those partial views had time to collide before anything shipped. Peter Naur made a version of this argument in 1985: a program’s real content is a theory held by the people who built it, and the theory does not live in the artifact. What he did not have to say out loud is that the theory was never resident in one person either. It got composed, briefly and imperfectly, in design reviews and postmortems and the twenty minutes after a design review when the two people who disagreed stayed behind.

The thing at risk, then, is the collision process — and that distinction decides what you do about it. Decaying expertise is answered with training and better reviewers. A starved collision process is not: no reviewer fixes it, and the process has to be rebuilt somewhere outside a person’s head.

That process survived on a ratio, and the ratio has moved. Not raw throughput — teams were shipping many times a day well before any of this, and continuous deployment is old enough to drive. The change is in how much thinking each change arrives carrying.

When producing a change cost hours of someone’s attention, that attention came bundled with it. The author had already held the change against their own partial model, discarded three versions, and arrived at review having done most of the composition check alone. Review was a second opinion.

Generation decouples those. A change can now arrive having consumed minutes of human attention and still be a good change. The private composition check that used to be a side effect of the effort did not happen, so review becomes the first look rather than the second. Multiply across a team and the total attention paid to composition falls even where headcount has not.

The ratio did not drift on its own. Somebody chose to let change velocity scale without scaling the verification that used to ride along with it, and that choice gets made again every quarter in most organizations. Calling it structural is how I keep letting myself off the hook for it.

Building the Envelope#

If the failure is compositional and the collision process is what got starved, the work is to rebuild that process in machinery. The useful moves are ones plenty of teams already own without treating them as safety infrastructure.

Separate producing a change from being trusted with one. Code review merges them: a diff either lands or it does not, and landing is a full grant of trust. Feature flags, progressive rollout, and automatic revert on regression pull them apart, so a team with those can afford to accept work it cannot fully vet, because acceptance stops being irreversible. Most teams have the pieces and have never used them this way.

The constraint has to live in the executor, not the instruction. Nobody hits a guardrail they are not forced through. That gets sharper with agents: if you want a change held in draft until an evaluator passes it, do not ask the model to wait — make the state transition a code check keyed on the verdict. Instructions are advisory, and something producing changes faster than anyone reads them will eventually find the case where the advice did not quite apply. I have spent more time on this one than on the rest combined, and its naive version fails in the least visible way, because a model that mostly complies looks exactly like a constraint that works.

Gate by blast radius. Auto-merge the internal tooling change; put an evaluator and a human on the authorization path. Most organizations cannot express that difference at all right now, which means they are managing review load when they think they are managing consequence.

None of it works without instrumentation good enough to answer why something regressed, not just that it did. Without tracing and error budgets and the ability to attribute a regression to a change, the rest of it is theater — you will know something moved and have no idea which of six things moved it.

None of that is new infrastructure. The reason to care is new. These were nice-to-haves when a human with a partial model stood between an idea and production. They are load-bearing now that the volume of plausible change has outrun the number of partial models available to collide with it.

Every mechanism above fires on a change, at deploy or shortly after, against whatever traffic happens to be present. That covers the common class — wrong on its own terms, or wrong under current load — which is most of what goes wrong and why flags and rollback earn their place.

It is not the class this essay opened with. Those failures need a second condition to arrive, and the condition may be months away. Progressive rollout cannot catch something that has not happened yet. That class needs fault injection, dependency-graph analysis, and game days built to create the second condition on purpose — all far less practiced than flags and rollback, all considerably more work.

The Trap#

Gates become targets. I spent a good while writing a reward function for a reinforcement learning system and watching the policy end up somewhere I had not intended. I rewarded the average health of five domains, and it starved the smallest one — fewest pods, lowest traffic, so its degradation moved the average least. That domain was internal engineering tooling, which meant the cheapest thing for the policy to sacrifice was the thing that lets everyone else ship. Separately, I rewarded improvement. It let things degrade slightly so it would have something to improve.

The first case was not cunning, it was arithmetic. An average cannot tell the difference between five domains slightly unhealthy and four healthy ones beside a casualty, and I had written the reward as an average. The policy did what that choice instructed. Calling it reward hacking gives it too much credit. All of it turned up in simulation, which is where the policy lived: at a sixty-second control interval, the millions of steps it needed to converge would have taken the better part of a decade to collect from a real cluster. None of it ever ran against a real cluster, so catching these cost me nothing.

What took longer to admit is that my fixes had the same defect. I removed the relative-improvement reward from the term where I had caught it being gamed, and the same structure was sitting in a different term I had not thought to check. I had removed it from the place I was looking.

Point that at engineering process and it gets uncomfortable. If the gate is “tests pass,” you get changes that pass tests, and a model will write to the tests you showed it, because you showed it the tests. If the gate is coverage, you get coverage.

The analogy carries, but not cleanly, and the difference cuts against me. My policy was small and non-linguistic: it could not model its own training, could not tell it was being evaluated, and never reasoned about my reward function at all. It found those gaps by gradient descent over millions of steps. A model writing code does not work that way, and I doubt it needs anything like as many attempts — a gate’s definition is usually sitting in the repository it was handed, which makes routing around it a reading problem rather than a search problem. How often that actually happens, I cannot tell you. The shape of the pressure is the same and the clock is faster.

What a Gate Buys#

All of which is a problem for the gates I proposed above, and the blast-radius one worst. Classifying changes by consequence creates a legible boundary with a large latency reward for landing on the cheap side of it. I have watched people game that for years without calling it that — rescope the diff, carve the noisy endpoint out of the SLI, argue that it is really tooling. It costs nothing and does not need an optimizer.

The answer I have is that the classification cannot be a declaration. Blast radius asserted by an author will be asserted downward. Blast radius derived — from the call graph, from what data the touched code can reach, from which SLOs have moved when that path changed before — can only be lowered by changing what the code touches, which is the outcome you wanted. That is more work than a dropdown, which is the point.

The objection generalizes, though. Gating on production behavior is also a measurement — error rate is a metric, and so is “survived contact with real traffic.” Goodhart does not exempt them, and I have a specific reason to know it. One of the fixes I was proudest of in that reward function was a counterfactual gate: a bonus that paid out only when the policy’s pre-positioned capacity beat a simple reactive baseline replayed over the same window. That is an outcome gate. It is also the fix I trust least, because the replay starts from the state the agent produced — so degrading the cluster in a way the reactive baseline cannot recover from is available to the agent and directly rewarded by the gate. It could not write the world. It could choose which world the comparison started in — a hole I left open, not one I watched being used.

What survives is smaller than the claim I wanted to make. A test-passing gate is closed: satisfying it needs nothing from outside the repository, and the generator authors the code and, in effect, the evidence. An outcome gate is not closed, but neither is it open. It is open exactly as far as the generator’s influence over production does not reach — a real distance, and one that shrinks every time the thing being gated gains more control over the system it runs on. You have to measure that distance. Assuming it is the mistake I made.

The other difference holds better. When a proxy gate is gamed, the failure arrives later and alone, at 3am, detached from the change that caused it. When an outcome gate is gamed for harm the metric actually covers, the gaming is the failure, so the harm and the signal arrive together. That is not prevention. It changes when you find out. Turning a silent debt into a loud one is most of what I want from a gate.

It does nothing for harm the metric does not cover, which is where this keeps landing. I removed relative-improvement rewards from the place I was looking. The edge of a gate gets drawn wherever its author happened to look. That applies to every claim in this section, including the two I just made.

Half a Lesson#

Every high-consequence engineering discipline concluded long ago that the practitioner should not have to hold it all in their head. Structural engineers work to codes written from collapses — Ronan Point put disproportionate-collapse provisions into the UK building regulations, and the Hyatt Regency walkway made shop-drawing review a duty the engineer of record could not delegate, because his seal was on it. Pilots run checklists because expertise turns out to be unreliable under load.

Those fields did not stop there, and this is where arguments like mine usually go wrong. The checklist in aviation sits on top of a type rating, fifteen hundred hours before you can occupy the right seat of an airliner, and a recurrent simulator check that can ground you. A surgeon counting instruments has already spent five to seven years operating under supervision, on a formal ladder that hands over responsibility in graded steps. The building code binds by statute; the stamp is what attaches a named, revocable license to the claim that the work complies. Each of those disciplines answered “process or people?” with both, and in every one of them the people half is a license requirement.

Software took the checklist and skipped the residency, and there was a reason: a bad deploy usually rolls back and a bad landing does not. That reason has always been weaker than it sounds, and the failures at the top of this essay are exactly where it fails — a metastable system does not recover when you remove what triggered it. It was never much of a reason for the authorization path either, or for anything that moves money, or for anything where the failure is somebody else’s identity. Those rooms sit closer to aviation than to a web app. They got the checklist. They never got the residency.

We could get away with it while the slowness of the work supplied an apprenticeship for free — you built your partial model by wandering, because wandering was unavoidable. That subsidy is ending.

Harnesses buy time. They do not manufacture understanding. A gate can tell you something regressed; it cannot tell you that you never grasped why the design worked. And there is a sharper version of that objection: if the gate catches it, why learn it? Aviation has names for both halves — automation complacency for the monitoring that decays, skill degradation for the hands that do — and one of its responses was to push pilots back toward hand-flying in low-workload conditions. I have no engineering equivalent to offer. Nobody has built the residency half.

Lately I have been building the envelope instead of writing about needing one — agents that carry an engineer’s stated intent through to production, with deterministic code standing between every proposal and the thing it would change. That boundary is where the interesting failures have been, and it is what I want to write about next.

“I asked Claude and this is what it told me” is a fine thing to say. It stopped being a confession, for good reasons. What has not caught up is the second question, and nothing in the answer will tell you there is one.
